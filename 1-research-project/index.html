Experience Research

Chapter 01 — Personal Interaction

01

What was the first thing you paid attention to when interacting with the experience?

The first thing that caught my attention was the rough, abstract line drawing that I could not immediately recognise. I then noticed the interaction inviting me to turn on the music and the oversized typography responding to my mouse movement. The text bounced across the screen and changed in scale as I interacted with it. Once the classical music began, it immediately reinforced the subject of the concert hall and made the opening feel more atmospheric and immersive. Together, the sound, typography and responsive motion created a strong first impression, and I was so focused on these elements that I actually noticed the “Scroll for story” instruction quite late.

03

What part of the experience did you spend the most time engaging with?

I spent the most time in “Explore the Model.” I could freely rotate the 3D architectural model and examine it from different directions. When I selected a specific point, the camera automatically moved towards it before connecting that area to related design images and information. This made the model feel less like a visual object to look at and more like a space I could actively investigate.

04

What was the most common action in your two minute interaction with the experience?

Vertical scrolling was my most common action, followed by moving and hovering with the mouse. Scrolling does more than move between sections; it controls the progression of images, text, models and videos. Because most of the experience can be navigated this way, I rarely had to stop and think about what control to use next.

Chapter 02 — Purpose & Structure

05

What is your impression of the intended primary goal of the interactive experience?

I think the primary goal is to communicate how Frank Gehry’s early ideas developed through sketches, models and design experiments into the final architecture. Rather than asking the user to understand technical drawings or long explanations, the website turns this process into a visual story. It therefore feels less like a conventional informational website and more like an interactive presentation of Gehry’s design process.

06

How does the interactive experience communicate this primary goal?

The website communicates this mainly by transforming one type of material into another through smooth transitions. Rough sketches become architectural forms, which then connect to models, photographs and videos. Instead of explaining every relationship through text, the transitions allow the user to visually understand how one stage of the design develops into another. More detailed information is separated into “Read More” sections, allowing the main narrative to remain clear and focused.

07

What is your impression of how the experience should be interacted with over time?

I think the experience is designed to be followed from beginning to end on the first visit rather than quickly searched for isolated information. The continuous narrative makes more sense when experienced in sequence. However, users who become interested in a particular part of the project can later return to specific chapters and explore the additional material in more depth. This gives the website both an overall narrative and a second, more detailed level of exploration.

08

How does the interactive experience communicate how it should be interacted with over time?

The website creates a clear sense of progression by dividing the story into visually distinct chapters. Each chapter has its own theme colour, combined with large black typography, so I could immediately recognise when I had entered a new stage of the story. The menu provides direct access to these chapters for returning visitors, while “Read More” allows users to decide when they want to move beyond the main narrative. The limited number of controls also keeps the first journey simple and guided.

Chapter 03 — Media References

09

What other media forms, digital or otherwise, does the experience reference?

The experience reminded me of architecture magazines, exhibitions and documentary films. Its large typography, generous spacing and carefully composed image layouts resemble editorial design, while the sequential use of video and transitions feels cinematic. The presentation of sketches, physical models and design studies also resembles the way architectural work might be curated in a gallery or exhibition.

10

What does this reference communicate about how you should act when engaging with your research experience?

These references encouraged me to behave more like a visitor moving through an exhibition than someone searching a database. I followed the sequence at the pace set by the scrolling experience, stopping when something caught my attention. Features such as “Read More” and the interactive model then gave me the choice to investigate particular subjects more closely without interrupting the main journey.

11

What does this reference communicate about how you should feel when engaging with your research experience?

The experience made architectural information feel curated, spacious and approachable rather than technical or overwhelming. The consistent theme colours, restrained use of black and white, and carefully controlled layouts gave the website a strong visual identity that reminded me of a designed exhibition. At the same time, the gradual revealing of Gehry’s ideas created curiosity because I was discovering relationships between different pieces of material rather than receiving all the information at once.

Chapter 04 — Evaluation

12

What is the most frustrating part of the interaction to you and what makes that part frustrating?

The most frustrating part was noticing the “Scroll for story” instruction relatively late. The opening screen contains several visually dominant elements and mouse-responsive interactions, so my attention initially went towards experimenting with them rather than finding the main navigation cue. Once I understood that scrolling was the primary interaction, the rest of the experience became very intuitive.

13

What is the most satisfying part of the interaction to you and what makes that part satisfying?

The most satisfying part was how naturally interaction was connected to understanding the architecture. Transitions helped me recognise relationships between different stages of the design, while the fixed-image layouts allowed me to compare detailed images without losing sight of the main subject. “Explore the Model” was especially satisfying because selecting a point physically repositioned the model before revealing related material. Instead of interaction being added only for visual effect, it consistently helped me understand the project.
